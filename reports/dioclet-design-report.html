The gameThe palace in one minute

Four players enter a ruined Roman palace through four gates. Each wants the same thing: gear up in the abandoned city, then race through the palace halls to be the first to strike down the Emperor, twice.

Level 1, the city. Six blocks hide one treasure card each. You roll and move, reveal cards, and take their treasure: a dagger, a shield, a sword… A full kit of six makes you deadlier in every fight. Along the way you can duel rivals within reach, or stand a guard post turn for guaranteed Glory if you roll well.

The checkpoint. The first fully equipped player opens the gate to Level 2. Anyone missing gear gets the rest for free, so nobody is left in the city. The first player through earns a bonus Glory.

Level 2, the palace. You need two different seals before the Emperor will face you: roll for the Cellars, fight the guards at the Barracks, or simply buy the Peristyle seal with Glory. Then it is two clean hits on the Emperor to win. A miss throws you back a step; a duel won pays Glory, and Glory buys power.

The engine roomGlory: one currency, real tension

The best thing the game has is its economy. Glory is scarce (you can hold only 2), easy to lose (spend it or waste it at the cap), and it buys exactly two things, both worth winning for.

MethodHow we tested, in plain words

Every rule change went through the same discipline, so results are comparable across the whole project:

  1. Scripted players first. Four simple player types (collector, fighter, all-rounder, wanderer) played 1,000 games per rule variant, always on the same board layouts, with every seat rotated fairly. Free, fast, and repeatable.
  2. Role-played games. Eight full games played character-by-character (cautious merchant, duelist, tactician, racer…) to catch what counters miss: how rules feel.
  3. AI players last. Language-model players that reason over the same public information a human sees. 160 games against a strong scripted benchmark, for a total cost of 54 cents.
  4. The physical board last. The designer's printed board was encoded cell by cell and every number re-measured on it. The designer's correction then set its final shape: six blocks, one card each (V0.6 to V0.7).
  5. One knob at a time. Each variant changed exactly one rule. Adopted changes became the new base for the next test.

Every game in every batch can be replayed move by move and reproduces exactly. The numbers below are verifiable, not estimated.

Fairness climbed, comebacks grew

…but aggression still does not pay

The two-hit Emperor ending works. Every qualitative table, scripted run, and AI game agrees: the countdown after a leader's first hit is the emotional peak of the game. Paid strikes (about 72% success) open the endgame; free strikes (about 45%) decide it when Glory runs dry. That arc survived every rule change we tested.

Findings · part 2What the AI players revealed

Reasoning players, given exactly the information a human sees, found strategies the scripts never tried, and walked past an obvious one.

QuestionAnswer from 160 AI games
Are they strong?On par with our best scripted benchmark. In 64 direct head-to-head games the script's seats won 39 to the model's 25: a lean, but not yet a proven gap.
New strategies?Two. The Glory engine: skip collecting almost entirely, win duels for Glory, and let the free catch-up gear finish your kit, then out-spend everyone at the Emperor. And seal-market timing: buy the guaranteed seal early, roll only for the cheap ones.
Blind spots?One glaring: guard posts (the board's only reliable Glory source) were used about once per 176 player-games. Players who ignored Glory lost locked-out and hoarding: one in five losers died holding unspent Glory.
Discipline?Flawless execution: a winning Emperor strike was taken 100% of the time (168 of 168 chances), with zero illegal moves across every game.
What this means for the design. The Glory engine works only because the free catch-up gear subsidizes it. That is a feature today (a legitimate second strategy), but if human players copy it, the city phase stops mattering. The fix under consideration: make the checkpoint require, for example, four of six treasures.

Findings · part 3The one problem left

You cannot slow a leader. A duel pays only its winner, so attacking the player about to win risks handing them Glory. Every qualitative table refused to do it; the AI players refused too, even in games where a rival sat one hit from victory. Three separate test cycles converged on the same root cause: what a duel pays the winner matters more than what it costs the loser.

Tested and failed: making the loser skip a turn (scripts liked it, reasoning players simply refused to aim it at leaders), stealing Glory (collapses the fighting style entirely), and pushing the loser to the retreat hall (a free teleport toward the Emperor).

The evidence now points at duel value: paying a bounty when you beat a player who holds an Emperor hit, or paying Glory for guard-post success. Either makes "fight the leader" rational for the first time. Both are single, small rule changes, and the next test cycle is cheap.

Second, smaller issue: the player who acts first in the final round still holds an edge (about 28% vs 21% of wins across 1,000 games). Fix candidates if human play shows it matters: a snake-shaped turn order, or all players strike at the Emperor simultaneously.

RecommendationsWhat to do next

  1. Lock the current package for the physical prototype. The six-block board with one card per block, two-dice movement, card entries spread north and south, the seal triangle, attacker wins ties, one grace round, rotating start, and the 24-round city cap. Every piece is tested; the only new component is a second die.
  2. Keep the Peristyle seal at 1 Glory. Doubling it did not create harder choices; it only locked poor players out.
  3. Prototype the leader bounty. One rule: beat a player who holds an Emperor hit in a duel and earn 2 Glory. It is the first mechanism that makes interference rational. Test it exactly like everything here: scripts, then AI players.
  4. Consider a collection requirement at the checkpoint (four of six treasures) so the Glory engine cannot fully replace the city phase; decide after human playtests.
  5. Playtest with people now; the pack is printed and waiting. The print-and-play pack (board, cards, trackers, rulebook, teaching script, feedback form) plus a session protocol exist in the repository. The simulator cannot answer the two feel questions: is half of all turn time spent walking too much, and does the city tail drag? Only a table can.
  6. Use the web board to teach it. The same measured ruleset plays in any browser, hot-seat humans against bot opponents. It is the fastest way to put the game in front of someone.
  7. Watch the final-round initiative. If human games show the first-player edge, test the snake order or simultaneous Emperor strikes before printing.

The bottom line

Across 29,000 scripted games, eight role-played games, and 160 AI games, the core promise held every time: a decisive, comeback-rich race with a real economy and a tense finish, now measured on the real six-block board. One structural gap remains: you cannot fight a leader. Three test cycles have isolated its cause, and the next candidate fix is a single sentence of rules text.

Verdict: worth developing into a physical product. Prototype after the leader-bounty test comes back positive; the testing rig to prove it already exists and costs pennies per hundred games to run.

Annex · prototype rulebookThe rules as they stand

This is the complete rule set that survived testing, version V0.7: the real six-block board. Written to be played as-is at a table. Every rule in it earned its place; the evidence table at the end shows what was tried and rejected on the way.

What you need

  • The palace board: the six-block city plan on this page, 97 street circles.
  • Four pawns, one per player, starting on the four gates.
  • Two ordinary six-sided dice for movement (add them together) and one six-sided die for every fight and trial.
  • Treasure cards: dagger, javelin, helmet, shield, sword, armor, one hidden at each of the six block entries.
  • Glory tokens (each player may hold at most 2).
  • Seal tokens in three kinds: Cellars, Barracks, Peristyle.
  • Two hit markers per player for the Emperor battle.

Goal and setup

Be the first to land two hits on the Emperor. Choose gates freely; all four are equally good (measured, see evidence below). Shuffle the six treasure cards face down, one per block entry A to F; nobody knows which block holds which treasure. Each player starts with no treasure and no Glory. Pick a starting player at random; the start rotates one seat clockwise every round.

On your turn

  1. Roll both movement dice. The sum is your maximum steps, not an exact count.
  2. Move. Walk along the street circles up to your roll. You may stop early, and you may pass through other pawns. Walking past someone never starts a fight.
  3. Do one thing at the square you reached (see the action lists below), or simply end your move. One action per turn, never two.

If you are already standing on a special square, you may use its action without moving (except guard posts and duels, which always require a move of at least one step).

Level 1: the city

ActionHow it works
Take a treasureEnd your move on a block's entry circle (A to F, on the north and south streets) and take that block's card. When a card is first taken it is revealed to everyone. Each treasure can be taken by every player once. Cards are never used up, and treasure is never stolen or lost.
Stand a guard postMove onto a guard post (★, on the west and east walls) and roll the die: 4, 5 or 6 earns 1 Glory. This is the board's reliable way to earn Glory; use it.
Duel a rivalEnd your move within two street spaces of another pawn to challenge them. Both roll a die and add a gear bonus (below). Higher total wins; ties go to the attacker. The winner takes 1 Glory. Nobody loses anything else: no gear, no position, no turns.
Claim the checkpointThe first player to own all six treasures and reach the gold checkpoint in the center claims it: take 1 Glory. This opens the gate to the palace (see below). Only the first claim counts.

Gear bonus: 0-2 treasures +0 · 3-5 treasures +1 · all 6 treasures +2. Added to every duel, Barracks contest, and Emperor strike.

Glory: you may hold at most 2. Anything earned beyond that is lost. Spend it or waste it.

Moving up to the palace

When the checkpoint is claimed, finish that round, then play one more full round in the city, a last chance to grab treasure, Glory, or a grudge match. Then:

  • Every player still missing treasures receives them for free, so nobody is left behind.
  • All pawns return to their gates, Glory is kept, and the palace phase begins. Turn order keeps rotating as before.

If nobody claims the checkpoint by the end of round 24, the city is abandoned and everyone moves up anyway, with the same free gear.

Level 2: the palace

The blocks change roles (see the board): the Cellars at the northwest entry A and the Barracks at the southeast entry F sell seals, the Peristyle at the center of the main street sells a guaranteed one, and the Emperor waits in his chamber at the south gate. You need any two different seals before you may face him. You cannot repeat a seal you already own, and duels remain available exactly as in the city.

SealHow to earn it
CellarsRoll the die at the Cellars: 3 or higher earns the seal.
BarracksRoll off against the guard at the Barracks: you roll die + gear bonus, the guard rolls die +1. Beat the guard strictly to earn the seal.
PeristyleRoll the die at the Peristyle: 5 or 6 earns the seal, or simply pay 1 Glory for it, no roll. The safe buy.

The Emperor

  1. Stand in his chamber and strike: you roll die + gear bonus, he rolls die +2. Score strictly higher to hit.
  2. You may declare a powered strike before rolling: pay 1 Glory for +2 to your roll, paid whether you hit or miss. A powered strike lands about 7 times in 10; a free one about 45 in 100.
  3. Hit: mark it, stay in the chamber, strike again next turn. Miss: step back one space to the retreat hall beside his gate. Nothing else is lost.
  4. First to two hits wins immediately.

What everyone may see

Everything is public: positions, treasure, Glory, seals, hits, and all revealed cards. The only secrets are the face-down treasure cards and the next die roll.
