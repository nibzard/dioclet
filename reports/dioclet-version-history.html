How to read this report

  • Every batch is deterministic: fixed seeds, one rule knob changed per variant, and the same SPEC §13 policy schedule, so differences come from the rule change.
  • Bot policies are frozen (DECISIONS.md D24): balanced, greedy, aggressive, random, and the V0.4 tactical benchmark. Frozen bots are heuristics: differences under about two points are noise, and 1,000 games resolve a 25% rate to about ±3 points (FINDINGS.md §9).
  • Live AI play used OpenAI Decisions with the model gpt-6-luna, budget caps enforced before every call, and full offline replay verification. Mock-provider games are never counted as model results.
  • Qualitative sessions were role-playing agent tables, not humans; their notes carry caveat banners where an engine bug was later fixed.
  • Nothing here is a fun score. The simulator screens mechanics and strategies; whether the game is clear, tense, and worth replaying is a table question the playtest protocols still have to answer.

Engine bug found and fixed during the study. A failed Emperor attack set the retreat position, then overwrote it with the action destination, so misses cost no tempo. Three independent playtest agents found it by reading logs; the original unit test passed because it returned early on wins. Fixed, covered by a stronger test, and every batch in the findings was re-run (FINDINGS.md §0).

Verdict. The baseline stands as the reference: gates fair within noise, dice not dominant, comebacks common — and turn order, not gates, is the real inequality. Aggression does not pay: the defender-biased tie rule plus lost tempo beats the 1-Glory duel reward.

FINDINGS_V03.md
  • Glory theft — the most counter-intuitive result of the study. Zero-sum transfer punished exactly the strategy it was meant to reward: aggressive fell to 0 wins in 500 seat-games while the never-dueling greedy policy soared to 36%. A duel against a Glory-empty victim pays nothing; pacifists win the Glory war.
  • Displacement — falsified by geometry. The loser drops on the retreat node, one step from the Emperor chamber: a doorstep teleport. In the narr8 playtest the door-guarder refused to kick a two-seal rival for exactly that reason, and losing a duel became win-win. Across 1,000 bot games, 108 chamber evictions delayed zero Emperor attempts.
  • Lost turn — the only consequence with teeth. Losing a duel costs tempo, the resource the race actually spends; aggressive reached its best result anywhere (14%). Adopt candidate, with one caution: a lost turn is the harshest-feeling consequence for children — a question only humans can settle.

Carried base result: the adopted package shortened games (median 14 rounds), raised comebacks to 70%, cut the first-challenger edge to 30%, and narrowed the seat gap to 28/21.

V0.4OpenAI Decisions player intelligence ✓ adopted2026-11-07 · ai_driver.py, decisions_policy.py

An observation-only AI seat: the model sees only the public observation and a audited candidate shortlist (2–24 options; winning attacks, seals, explores, and every distinct duel target always kept; exact d6×d6 odds only — no invented long-horizon probabilities). A hidden sentinel planted in engine state never appeared in any request. Call, cost, token, and wall-clock caps are enforced before each request; exhaustion marks the game incomplete, never a draw.

  • Tactical benchmark bot (frozen as tactical-v04.1): 31.6% over 2,750 seat-games — competent but not dominant; it fixes the aggressive trap by ranking winning Emperor attacks above duels.
  • Contract checks (3 live calls): schema matched exactly; the endgame fixture chose the winning attack at 0.91 confidence while early turns sat honestly at 0.25.
  • Live pilots (3 games, ≈$0.0135 total): legal, clean, win-seeking play; per-turn cost about $0.0001 and 0.2 s median after warm-up. Explicitly labeled pilots, not performance estimates.

V0.4.1AI tournament and decision analysis measured, nothing adopted2026-11-07 · 64 live games, $0.319 of $1.00

Sixty-four live gpt-6-luna games (32 mirror + 32 mixed), 2,845 decisions, zero refusals, zero invalid answers, all replay-verified offline.

  • Strength: tactical 15/48 vs decisions 12/48 mixed seat-wins — overlapping intervals. Decisions plays at roughly tactical's strength; both exploit the same two edges.
  • Clean play: winning-attack utilization 92/92 = 100%; zero unproductive explores; the main leak is Glory hoarding (19% of losses held unspent Glory).

  • Confidence is choice uncertainty: mean 0.27; 0.19 in Level 1 vs 0.44 in Level 2; low confidence never produced an erratic pick in 2,845 tries.

Five game studies (notes/v041/):

  • ta05 — the stall. 26 rounds; four AI players simultaneously failed to rank the one progress action for ten rounds while all sat capped at 5 of 6 items. Motivated the Level 1 cap.
  • ta07 — combat without conversion. 14 duels, all at the best edge on offer; P2 and P3 won five duels between them and attempted the Emperor zero times. The best duelist lost; the selective one won.
  • ta14 — the close endgame. Five attacks, five hits; the 42% unboosted winner took the crown. Verdict line: initiative first, skill second, dice third.
  • tb202 — the window failure. The decisions seats never built Glory, so they got 1 Emperor swing to tactical's 4; their failed interference duels funded the winner's seals.
  • tb209 — the Glory engine. A dominant decisions win: 5–0 in duels, perfect Emperor conversion, Glory timing lifting attacks from 42% to 72%.

Two findings. Single d6 cannot finish the city phase on the real board (94% timeouts). And content placement is a fairness lever as strong as any rule: entries on the west and east streets hand those gates 44% each; spreading entries equalizes the first-challenger race, but clustering both roll-seal sites on the north street then hands north the Level 2 race — hence the seal triangle (cellars northwest, barracks southeast, Peristyle center).

Encoding audit.   • The board is fair; the skew was in the bots. Four 1,000-game mirror probes: random×4 splits gates 23/24/22/23, while the frozen scripted bots break ties toward the northwest — the balanced_se probe (ties southeast) flips the skew with them. Humans and reasoning players show no fixed direction.

Era IV — imperial succession (V0.8 – V0.8.1)

V0.8The redesign and its motivation 2026-11-08 · docs/intervention-plan.md

Why redesign. The user found seals complicated and weakly motivated, and asked for meaning drawn from the Tetrarchy and the struggle after Diocletian's retirement (notes/faction-support.md, with historical sources on the Tetrarchy and Constantine's proclamation by his troops). The working rule from the history notes: a fact sticks when play makes it happen — relabeling the same three tests would not answer the request. A separate incentive analysis (notes/treasure-incentives.md) showed free transition equipment weakens the collection race: the auto-grant made a 2-item kit identical to a 6-item kit at the transition.

What changed. Seals became faction support (visit a meeting place, pay 1 Prestige; two of three factions qualify a claim). Glory became Prestige with spending options, capped at 3. Equipment persists through the whole game; no free grants; collection stays legal in succession. The Emperor boss fight became a contested claim at the Peristyle that every rival answers. Challenging an active claimant costs a fee, paid before the roll. The board is the V0.7.3 six-block layout, with named players and seeded gate assignment.

The palace contest between four claimants is fiction inspired by real history — the notes say so explicitly, and the rulebook carries the same honesty.

Playtests — the qualitative record

Eight narrative games were played by role-playing agent tables through the same CLI loop, one action per turn, observations only. They are qualitative signal, not measurement. Games 1–5 ran on the engine with the retreat bug; their notes carry caveat banners.

narr1 — the pacifist table

Zero duels in 55 turns; once everyone holds the same tier, a duel is a coin flip the defender wins. Seat order, not skill, broke the endgame tie.

narr2 — the duelists

Smart aggression beat pure aggression: arming to three items first, then bullying. The Glory cap was the loudest complaint — the best brawler's wins paid nothing.

narr3 — four personas, one bug

Ties to the defender decided three of eleven duels, all against one player. This table found the emperor-miss retreat bug by reading the logs.

narr4 — the flattened race

A player who explored nothing entered Level 2 identical to one who explored everything: the auto-grant erased the whole Level 1 scorecard.

narr5 — the endgame window

The seal race was fast and clean; the miss-retreat never fired (the bug); the tense part was the race to two hits, decided 9–5.

narr6 — the fix bites

On the corrected engine a miss costs Glory, position, and a full turn of tempo. "The Emperor window feels earned rather than a free re-roll lottery."

narr7 — interference hunger

Every persona independently refused to duel a leader: duels take nothing from the loser and only pay the winner. The last three turns were watched, not played.

narr8 — the doorstep teleport

Under displacement, players farmed the kick that was meant to punish them, because the drop point is one step from the goal. Falsified the geometry.

Protocols, written and waiting. The V0.6.1 protocol prescribes four groups (children 6–9 with a facilitator, a mixed-age family, adult strategy players, older non-gamers) with timed teaching, solo feedback forms, and recorded observations only — predictions never written into recorded fields. The V0.8 protocol adds a goal check (each player explains unaided how someone wins) and a four-game gate rotation. A session counts as positive evidence only if players can state the goal, name a real choice they made, and want another game. No human session has been recorded.

The final game — V0.8.1 rules in full

The current game on the V0.7.3 six-block board, released with a contest fee of 1 Prestige. Everything below is read from rules_v08_succession.json and the shared rulebook text in tools/succession_content.py.

Overview and goal

Diocletian has retired. Four rivals prepare a claim to imperial power: collect equipment, earn Prestige, secure faction support. When succession begins, reach the Peristyle with two supporters and proclaim your claim. If it survives one response turn from every rival, you are acclaimed emperor. The rulebook's own provenance line: "Succession prototype V0.8. The starting economy is experimental."

Setup

  1. Lay out the six-block board: six 5×5 blocks on a 19×13 grid, six shuffled equipment cards, one per block, face down.
  2. Give each player a pawn, a player sheet, and a name and color. Names belong to players, not gates.
  3. Assign gates: seeded random (default), manual choice of four distinct gates, or rotate gates across four repeated games. Golden north, Silver east, Bronze south, Iron west.
  4. Place Prestige counters and the tracker cards in reach.

A turn

  1. Roll two six-sided dice for movement.
  2. Move along street circles up to the total; you may stop early and pass other pawns.
  3. Take one action at your destination. Collecting equipment, gaining support, and fighting are actions; a guard action or a duel also needs at least one space of movement.

The first player rotates every round. The city phase ends when a player with all six equipment types opens the checkpoint (finish that round plus one full grace round), or after 24 city rounds. At the transition, every pawn keeps its position and everything it holds; nothing is granted for free.

Duels and the contest fee

  • Each side rolls one die and adds its equipment bonus: 0–2 items +0, 3–5 items +1, all six +2. The attacker wins ties.
  • The winner earns 1 Prestige. No one loses equipment or support in a duel.
  • Before rolling, the attacker may pay 1 Prestige for +2 on the roll — paid even on a loss, and separate from the fee below.
  • Challenging an active claimant costs 1 Prestige, paid before the roll, win or lose. An ordinary duel against a player with no active claim is free. A winning challenge still earns the normal reward.
  • Why the fee exists, in one line of history: fee 0 collapsed into a free-denial loop; fee 2 silenced all counterplay in live games; fee 1 kept 12 of 13 live claims contested.

One rule for all three: reach the meeting place, pay 1 Prestige, gain the support — no roll, both phases, yours for the rest of the game. Every faction can back every player. Two different supporters qualify a claim; rivals do not need to endorse you.

The claim and the win

  1. During succession, reach the Peristyle with two supporters and proclaim your claim. Only one claim can be pending.
  2. Every rival gets one response turn: challenge you, prepare their own position, or act otherwise. Declining to challenge is a valid response.
  3. A winning challenge cancels your claim and returns your pawn to your assigned gate; your equipment and support remain.
  4. If all three rivals finish their responses with your claim intact, you are acclaimed emperor and win.

If nobody wins within 24 succession rounds, the game is a draw.
